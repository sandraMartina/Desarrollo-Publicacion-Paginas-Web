From the Field

A morning in the pine forest

January 1, 2021 by Mark

There's a particular quiet that settles over a pine forest just after sunrise, before the wind picks up and the birds get loud. This post walks through what to look, listen, and smell for on an early trail.

Dew still clings to the needles underfoot, and the light comes through the canopy in thin, shifting bands. Even a short walk at this hour rewards patience, so we've tried to capture that feeling here, along with a few practical notes on gear and pacing for anyone planning a similar outing.

Words worth carrying

A thought that stayed with us on this trip:

In every walk with nature, one receives far more than he seeks.

It's a line we kept coming back to on the descent, when the trail opened into a clearing and the whole valley came into view. Moments like that are the reason we keep coming back to the same stretch of forest, season after season..

What to pack

A short trip doesn't need much, but a few things make the difference between a comfortable hike and a miserable one. Here's what we bring on most day hikes:

  • Layered clothing for shifting mountain weather
  • A field notebook and a pair of compact binoculars
  • More water than you think you'll need

And a rough order for the day itself:

  1. Start before sunrise to catch the forest waking up
  2. Take the ridge trail for the best canopy views
  3. Rest by the creek before heading back down

A few terms that come up often in field notes:

Understory
The layer of shrubs and small plants growing beneath a forest's main canopy
Watershed
An area of land that drains into a single river or lake
Canopy
The uppermost layer of branches and leaves formed by a forest's tallest trees

Reading the trail

Learning to notice small details in the landscape takes practice. A few things worth training your eye for:

  • Animal tracks, pressed into soft mud near streams, often reveal more activity than you'd expect.
  • Bird calls, once you learn a handful, turn a silent-seeming forest into a busy one.
  • Lichen growth, like the pale green patches on north-facing bark, can help with orientation when the sky is overcast.
  • Fallen logs, left undisturbed, become homes for insects, fungi, and small mammals over many seasons.
  • Water clarity in a stream often signals the health of everything living upstream of it.

None of this requires special equipment, just a slower pace and a willingness to stop often.

Why we keep a field journal

Writing things down, even briefly, changes how closely you pay attention. A short entry after each hike is often enough to notice patterns across a season that would otherwise slip by unremarked.

Getting started

You don't need any special training to begin. A notebook, a pencil, and a regular trail are enough to start building a record of the seasons as they change around you.. 

Date, location, weather, species spotted

That's the whole template. Everything else is just detail you add as you go.

Life along the riverbank

December 23, 2020 by Jacob

Riverbanks are some of the busiest habitats around, packed into a narrow strip of land where forest meets water. This post covers what we found on a slow walk along one stretch of shoreline over a single afternoon.

The river seemed almost alive with the sound of insects and moving water.

Herons stood motionless in the shallows for long stretches, waiting on fish we couldn't see. Dragonflies moved in short, sharp bursts between the reeds, and every few minutes a fish broke the surface to catch one mid-air.

What we counted

A rough tally from the afternoon:

Species Sightings Notes
Great blue heron 3 Fishing near the bend
Painted turtle 7 Basking on a fallen log
Dragonfly 12+ Too many to count precisely
Total 22+

A single afternoon on one short stretch of river, and still more species than we could keep track of..

New trail added

December 14, 2020 by Chris

We've mapped and added a new loop trail through the eastern ridge, covering a stretch of mixed forest that's been closed to the public for the past two seasons while it recovered from storm damage.

  • Roughly 6 miles round trip, moderate difficulty
  • Passes two overlooks with valley views
  • Best hiked in early morning during warmer months

Trail markers are freshly placed, but conditions can still change quickly after rain, so check recent reports before heading out..
